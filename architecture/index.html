Insights / Architecture

Chicago architecture, from a buyer's point of view.

Architecture changes what a home is like to live in, what it costs to keep up, and what the next buyer will pay for it. This is a practical guide to Chicago's building types, from greystones to Mies.

By McGuire Price · October 2026

Chicago building types, and what they mean for an owner

Most Chicago homes fall into a handful of types. Each one has its own strengths and its own list of things to check.

TypeWhat it isWhat a buyer should know
Greystone Limestone-fronted two- and three-flats and houses, roughly 1890s to 1920s. Facades and back porches are the big-ticket items. Many have been deconverted to single-family homes.
Two-flat Two stacked units, usually brick, built across the city in the early 1900s. A common way to buy with rental income. Check that both units are legal and separately metered.
Chicago bungalow One-and-a-half-story brick homes from the 1910s to the 1930s. Solid construction and efficient plans. Attics and basements are where the added space usually comes from.
Vintage courtyard building U-shaped walk-ups around a garden court, mostly 1910s and 1920s. Light, character and generous rooms, usually no elevator. Masonry, roofs and the age of the systems matter most.
Workers' cottage Small 19th-century frame or brick houses, often on narrow lots. Often expanded over the years. Foundations and permits for past work are the key questions.
Prewar co-op Elegant apartment buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, mostly on the lakefront. You buy shares in a corporation, not real estate. Board approval and financing limits apply.
Mid-century tower Concrete or steel high-rises from the 1950s to the 1970s. Good plans and views. Window, facade and mechanical replacement cycles drive special assessments.
Timber loft conversion Former warehouses and factories converted to homes from the 1980s on. Exposed structure and high ceilings. Check the quality of the conversion, sound between units, and whether bedrooms meet code.
New construction Towers and small condo buildings built since the 2000s. The builder's track record, the warranty and the association's first budget and reserves matter most.

Architects whose buildings you can live in

Mies van der Rohe

Several of Mies's residential towers on and near Lake Shore Drive are condominiums, which makes Chicago one of the few places you can own a unit in a building that changed how the world builds. What buyers should know

Frank Lloyd Wright and the Prairie School

Most of Wright's Chicago-area houses are in Oak Park and the suburbs, and the best-known one in the city, the Robie House, is a museum. Prairie School homes by his contemporaries, including the Walter Burley Griffin houses in Beverly, come up for sale more often than Wright's own.

Louis Sullivan

Little of Sullivan's residential work survives. The Charnley-Persky House on Astor Street, designed with a young Wright, is now a museum. His influence shows up instead in the ornament of commercial buildings across the city.

Bertrand Goldberg

Marina City's two "corncob" towers on the river are condominiums. The curved, wedge-shaped units and the building's age make floor plan and condition especially important.

Buying in a historic district

Chicago has dozens of landmark districts, from the Old Town Triangle to Astor Street. Inside one, exterior work that needs a building permit (windows, additions, porches, masonry) is reviewed by the Commission on Chicago Landmarks. Interiors are generally not regulated.

A listing on the National Register of Historic Places is different: on its own, it generally doesn't restrict what a private owner does with private money. Illinois has also offered a property tax assessment freeze for owner-occupants who substantially rehabilitate a qualifying historic home. Check the current terms with the state historic preservation office before counting on it.
