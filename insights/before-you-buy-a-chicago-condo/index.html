Before you buy a Chicago condo: the documents to read and the questions to ask

The listing tells you about the unit. The association's documents tell you about the building you're also buying into. Here's what to read, and what to ask for that isn't required.

By McGuire Price · October 2026

The short answer

Before you're committed, read the association's budget, reserves, rules, insurance and recent board minutes, and ask about special assessments, upcoming projects, the share of units rented and any lawsuits. In Illinois, the seller has to provide much of this. The rest, you ask for.

What the seller has to give you

Section 22.1 of the Illinois Condominium Property Act requires the seller of a resale unit to make a set of documents available to a buyer who asks. Among them:

  • The declaration, bylaws, and rules and regulations
  • The current operating budget and the most recent financial statement
  • A statement of reserves, and of capital expenditures the board expects in the current and next two fiscal years
  • Current assessments and any special assessments
  • Any liens or unpaid assessments on the unit
  • A statement of pending lawsuits involving the association
  • The association's insurance coverage

In Chicago, these usually arrive during attorney review, the short window after an offer is accepted. That's tight. When I can, I start asking about the building before we write the offer.

What to ask for that isn't required

  • Board minutes from the last 12 to 24 months. They show what the board is worried about before it shows up in the budget.
  • The most recent reserve study, if the building has one. It estimates when big components (roof, masonry, elevators, windows) will need replacing and what that will cost.
  • Whether the building meets lender guidelines for conventional and FHA loans. This affects your financing and the next buyer's.
  • Who manages the building: a management company or the owners themselves.
  • Move-in rules, pet rules, leasing rules and any right of first refusal.

The questions I ask about every condo

  1. How much is in reserves, and when was the last reserve study?
  2. Have there been special assessments in the last five years, and are any planned?
  3. What major work is coming: roof, masonry, facade, elevators, windows?
  4. What share of units are rented, and is there a rental cap?
  5. Does the building meet lender requirements for conventional and FHA loans?
  6. Is the association involved in any lawsuits?
  7. What do the dues cover: heat, gas, internet, parking?
  8. Is the parking space deeded, leased or assigned?

Red flags worth slowing down for

  • Reserves that look small for the building's age and size
  • Repeated special assessments, or a big project in the minutes that isn't in the budget
  • Masonry, roof or facade problems discussed but not scheduled
  • Litigation, especially between the association and owners or a developer
  • A high share of rented units, which can complicate financing
  • Dues that seem too low for what they cover

None of these automatically means walk away. They mean find out more, and price it in.

Small buildings are different

A lot of Chicago condos are in three- to six-unit buildings run by the owners. Dues are often low, and there may be no reserve study or management company. That isn't a problem by itself, but the building's health depends on a few neighbors, and lenders often treat very small associations differently. Ask how decisions get made, and how the last big repair was paid for.

What this means if you're buying

Read the building like you read the unit. Two condos that look identical in photos can be very different purchases once you see the budget, the reserves and the minutes.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your real estate attorney reviews the condo documents and your contract, and the details depend on the building and the transaction.
