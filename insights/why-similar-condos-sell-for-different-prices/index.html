Why two similar condos in the same building sell for different prices

Same building, same square footage, very different sale prices. The difference is almost always in things a spreadsheet doesn't capture well.

By McGuire Price · October 2026

The short answer

Buyers pay for how a home lives, not just its size. Floor, exposure, view, floor plan, parking, outdoor space, condition and timing can all separate two units that look identical on paper.

The tier

In a high-rise, units stacked in the same vertical line share a floor plan, a view and an exposure. Some tiers simply live better: a corner, a split-bedroom plan, more wall space. Buyers who know the building know which tiers those are.

Floor and view

Higher usually sells for more, but not always in a straight line. A view that is open today can be blocked by a new building tomorrow, so a protected view (over a park, the lake or a low-rise historic block) is worth more than one across an empty lot.

Exposure

Which way the windows face changes the light, the heat and the feel of a room through the day. Two units with the same layout on opposite sides of a building can feel like different homes.

Floor plan

A layout that works with real furniture beats extra square footage that doesn't. Split bedrooms, a usable dining space, and storage that isn't in the hallway all show up in the price.

Parking and outdoor space

A deeded parking space, or a balcony or terrace you can actually use, is scarce in most of the city. Whether parking is included, deeded, leased or unavailable can account for a big share of the difference between two units.

Condition

Buyers budget for what they would have to fix, and they usually overestimate it. An updated kitchen and baths, newer windows or a newer heating and cooling system narrow that gap.

What the dues cover

Two buildings with the same monthly assessment can be very different deals if one includes heat, gas and internet and the other doesn't. Within a building, buyers compare dues against the unit's size and features too.

Timing and competition

When two similar units are on the market at the same time, they compete with each other. The one that's priced, prepared and presented better tends to sell first, and the other often adjusts.

What this means if you're selling

Pricing from the building's last sale isn't enough. The right price starts there and adjusts for what makes your unit different, in both directions. That's the case your listing has to make.

What this means if you're buying

A lower price isn't always a better deal. Ask why a unit is priced where it is, and compare it with the unit, not just the building.

This is general information, not legal or financial advice. Every building and every transaction is different.
