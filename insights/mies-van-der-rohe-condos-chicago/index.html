Living in a Mies van der Rohe building in Chicago: what buyers should know

Chicago is one of the few places where you can own a home in a building by Mies van der Rohe. Here's what makes those buildings different to live in, and what to ask before you buy.

By McGuire Price · October 2026

The short answer

Several of Mies's residential towers on and near Lake Shore Drive are condominiums you can buy into. They offer floor-to-ceiling glass, open plans and some of the most influential modern design anywhere. They also come with building-specific rules and maintenance costs that follow from that design, so ask about the windows, heating and cooling, renovation rules and how facade work is funded.

The buildings

These are the best-known Mies residential towers in Chicago that are owned rather than rented. Attribution and ownership structure can be more complicated than a listing suggests, so confirm the details for any specific building.

  • 860–880 N. Lake Shore Drive (Streeterville, 1951). The twin glass-and-steel towers that set the template for the modern high-rise. A Chicago Landmark.
  • 900–910 N. Lake Shore Drive (Streeterville, mid-1950s). The next generation, just north of 860–880.
  • Commonwealth Promenade (330–340 W. Diversey Parkway, mid-1950s). On the lakefront where Lincoln Park meets Lakeview.
  • 2400 N. Lakeview Avenue (Lincoln Park, 1963). A later tower facing the park.

What makes them different from a typical condo

  • The glass. Floor-to-ceiling windows give extraordinary light and views. They also mean more heat in summer, more cold in winter and less privacy than a masonry building.
  • The facade is the building. Steel frames need regular painting and repair, and glass and window systems eventually need replacing. Those are building-wide projects, paid for by the owners.
  • Uniformity is part of the design. Some of these buildings have rules that keep window coverings and other visible elements consistent, so the facade reads as one design.
  • Plans are open, but original kitchens and baths can be compact. What you can change depends on the structure and the building's rules.

Questions to ask before you buy

  1. When were the windows or glass last replaced or upgraded, and is more planned?
  2. What is the status of the building's facade inspection and repair program?
  3. How is the unit heated and cooled, and can you control it yourself?
  4. What renovations does the building allow, and what approvals are needed?
  5. Are there rules on window coverings or other visible elements?
  6. How large are reserves relative to the facade and window work ahead?
  7. Is the building a Chicago Landmark, and does that affect exterior work?

Does the name add value?

For the right buyer, yes: these buildings attract people who care about design and are hard to replicate. But the unit still has to work. Floor, exposure, plan, condition and the building's finances matter as much here as anywhere, and two units in the same Mies tower can be very different purchases.

What this means if you're buying or selling

If you're buying, read the building's maintenance history as carefully as you look at the view. If you're selling, the listing should explain the architecture well enough that the buyers who value it understand what they're looking at.

Building details here are general. Confirm the specifics for any building with its association and your attorney.
